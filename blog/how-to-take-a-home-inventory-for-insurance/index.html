How to Take a Home Inventory for Insurance (Step-by-Step)

Itemize Team · Updated September 2026 · 8 min read

Most people find out what a home inventory should have looked like at the worst possible time — a week after a fire, flood, or break-in, trying to remember what was in a drawer that no longer exists. Here's how to build one properly, before you need it.

Why "I know what I own" isn't good enough

You know what you own in the sense that nothing would surprise you. You do not know it in the sense an insurance adjuster needs: brand, model, approximate purchase date, condition, and proof it existed at all. After a loss, memory reconstructs the big things first — furniture, electronics, appliances — and quietly drops the rest: the contents of closets, the tools in the garage, the kids' bedrooms, the stuff in storage bins. Adjusters aren't being difficult when they ask for documentation; without it, they have no way to verify a claim beyond your word, and most policies are written around that limitation.

What actually holds up during a claim

An inventory that survives contact with an insurance claim has three things for each item, not just a description:

  • A photo. Not a group shot of a room — a photo of the item itself, ideally with any model/serial number visible if it has one.
  • An estimated value. What it would cost to replace, not what you paid for it years ago — most homeowners and renters policies pay out on a replacement-cost or actual-cash-value basis, and the adjuster will want a number to start from.
  • A location. Which room, and ideally where in that room. This matters more than people expect — it's what lets you sanity-check your own list months or years later ("wait, did I ever add the things in the hall closet?").

The room-by-room walkthrough

Trying to inventory a house as one giant list is how most attempts stall out. Go room by room, and within each room, go by area rather than by memory — dresser, then closet, then nightstand, rather than trying to recall everything in the bedroom at once. A few things make this faster:

  1. Open drawers and closets as you go. The items that get missed are never the TV in the middle of the room; they're the contents of things with doors.
  2. Group similar low-value items instead of itemizing each one. "12 books, estimated $180" is a legitimate inventory line. Save individual entries for anything with real value or anything irreplaceable.
  3. Do high-value rooms first. Home office, primary bedroom, and kitchen usually carry more insured value than the rest of the house combined — if you only get through part of the house in one sitting, that's where the coverage risk actually is.
  4. Don't skip the garage, attic, and storage. Tools, seasonal equipment, and sports gear add up to real money and are the areas people forget exist until a claim forces the question.

Where to actually keep it

A spreadsheet works until your phone is the thing documenting a fire — then it doesn't matter how organized the spreadsheet is if it lived on a laptop that didn't survive. The inventory needs to exist somewhere that survives the same event your house doesn't: cloud-synced, accessible from your phone, and backed up automatically rather than "backed up when I remember to export it."

Keeping it current instead of doing it once

A one-time inventory decays the moment you buy a new couch or your kid's bike gets swapped for a bigger one. The inventories that hold up over years are the ones updated as a habit — add an item when you buy it, the same way you'd keep a receipt. That's a much smaller task done fifty times a year than one exhausting weekend redone from scratch every few years, and it means the inventory is accurate on the day you actually need it, not accurate as of whenever you last got around to it.

What to export for an actual claim

When a claim happens, insurers generally want a written, itemized list — not just photos. Look for a way to export your inventory as a report grouped by room, with photos, descriptions, and values attached to each line, rather than sending an adjuster a folder of loose images and asking them to match things up.

Bottom line

A home inventory is worth the least the day you build it and worth the most the one day you need it — which is exactly why it has to be low-effort enough to actually maintain. Photo, value, location, per item, kept current as you go. Everything else is a nice-to-have.
